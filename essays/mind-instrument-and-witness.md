## How to read this

**Part 1 is a plain-language tour — no equations, just analogies.** If you have basic scientific training and have never touched this material, start there and you should be able to *reason* your way in, not just nod along. Everything after Part 1 is the same ideas with the rigor put back, for anyone who wants it. You can stop after Part 1 and have the whole shape.

I wrote the plain part first and deliberately, because most writing in this area asks you to already believe, or to "get it" by feel. I wanted a version you could follow with the ordinary rational equipment of someone who has taken a physics or a stats class.

## Part 1 — In plain language

### 1. The one question

Is consciousness a **light switch** or a **dimmer**?

Switch: you either have it or you don't, and somewhere in evolution humans (or maybe mammals) flipped it on. Dimmer: it's a continuous quantity — near-zero in a thermostat, low in an insect, higher in a dog, high in us — and nothing ever flipped, some systems just turned it up.

That's the whole question, and how you answer it changes what you think you are.

### 2. Two layers: the movie and the screen

Watch any moment of experience closely and it splits in two.

There's the **content** — thoughts, images, sensations, emotions, the ongoing "movie." And there's the **awareness the movie is showing up in** — call it the screen, or the light the movie is projected with.

Here's the part that takes a second to land: the "self" you normally defend — my name, my story, my personality, my plans — is a **character in the movie**. It's content. The awareness is not a character; it's what the movie is *playing on*.

The striking thing, and the reason this is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as mysticism, is that **wildly independent traditions — Buddhist, Hindu, Daoist, Greek, Jewish — each found this same split on their own** and each said the same warning: you keep mistaking the character for the screen.

Modern brain science draws almost exactly the same line, just with different names. It can explain the *movie* — how the brain integrates information, directs attention, builds a model of itself, reports on its own states. It cannot explain the *screen* — why there's any "showing up" at all, why all that information-processing is *lit* rather than happening in the dark. That gap has a name (the "hard problem"), and it is unsolved by everyone, including me.

### 3. The answer, in plain terms

Split the question along that seam and it resolves into two very different answers:

- **The screen (bare awareness) is probably not special to humans.** More dimmer than switch. This is the honest bet of most of the traditions and of the more serious modern "consciousness is fundamental" positions — but it *is* a bet, not a proven fact, because nobody has closed the question of why there's a screen at all.
- **What's special about humans lives in the movie.** Our movie contains a character that can **watch itself, notice its own patterns, and rewrite its own script** — and, apparently uniquely, **hand that script to the next generation** through language and culture. That's the real human distinctive: not a rarer kind of awareness, but a movie sophisticated enough to edit itself and be copied forward.

So: awareness, probably everywhere and graded; human specialness, a structural achievement *inside* the content, not a different substance of consciousness.

### 4. How the past grips the present: mud vs spring

A short detour that turns out to matter. History can hold onto a system in two very different ways.

- **Mud.** A drag that resists motion in proportion to how fast you're going. It slows you, it forgets, it never gives anything back.
- **Spring.** It *stores* what you push into it and *gives it back later*. It can overshoot, oscillate, carry momentum.

A habit modeled as mud is "feedback" — a bias that fades. A habit modeled as a spring is "inertia" — stored momentum that carries you even when you'd rather stop. From the inside they feel similar. They are mathematically different, and it turns out **most of what we casually call habit or "karma" is mud, not spring** — a fading drag, not real stored momentum. ([I worked this out carefully in a separate short physics piece]({{< relref "memory-inertia-feedback.md" >}}) — the one line that survives is: *forgetting is not momentum; to have real momentum, memory has to store and return.*)

Why does this belong here? Because it's a clean, small example of the whole method: take a vague intuition ("the past weighs on me like inertia"), put it in honest modern-scientific form, and find out it was *half* right and exactly which half. That is what I want from a framework.

### 5. The trap: mistaking the map for the territory

There's a failure mode with an old name — **所知障**, roughly "the obstruction of the knowable": when your knowledge *about* a thing piles up until it blocks direct contact *with* it. You can assemble a perfect library on swimming and never once get wet.

I hit this head-on. When I tried to unify all these traditions plus modern science into one clean scientific map, I ran an adversarial review against my own synthesis — and it showed that the map itself had become an instance of the trap. My tidy "movie vs screen" grid was partly something I had *imposed*: several traditions actually **reject** the split (some say both layers are equally empty; some say the "screen" is itself active, not a passive witness). Counting them all as agreeing was me finding my own assumption everywhere I looked. And the elaboration — the ever-neater table — had quietly started substituting for the looking.

The honest lesson, and the reason I'm keeping the failure in public: **a map of mind is a raft to cross on, not a monument to defend.** Cut the map, keep the practice.

### 6. So is a scientific framework even worth building? Yes — if the authority points the right way

Given all that, why build a framework at all? Because a raft is genuinely useful — *if* you keep the authority pointed correctly.

Simple test: **when your framework and your direct experience disagree, which one wins?** If you'd trust the map over your own feet, you're lost — the map has become a cage. If experience always gets the final vote, and the theory is only there to tell you where to look next, the theory is a good raft.

And building it in modern-scientific language specifically is not a betrayal of the traditions — it's the same move they made. Their maps (the Buddhist eight-consciousness model, the yoga-sutra model of mind, and so on) were built in each culture's sharpest available language *to guide practitioners*. Modern-scientific language is simply the sharpest one I actually own. There's one extra hazard, though, and it's worth naming: **scientific language loves to treat its models as the real thing.** It has no built-in "now drop the map" step the way the contemplative traditions do (they end with "and this teaching too is a raft"). So if you build in this language, you have to add the drop-the-map discipline *by hand*.

### 7. How to actually start looking

You can't get usable data from a shaky camera. The first job of practice isn't insight — it's **stabilizing the instrument**: training attention until it's steady enough to watch experience without stirring it up. (This is what the "just watch the breath" starter instruction is quietly for.)

Then a genuinely rational method opens up. Instead of asking *"what was I thinking about"* (content), you ask *"how did that thought arise — what came just before it, what was its texture, where did it go"* (structure). You replay a single moment in slow motion.

And there's a whole small science built on exactly this — **neurophenomenology** — which pairs these trained first-person reports with brain measurements so each corrects the other. It's the one part of this entire enterprise that is honestly, third-person *testable*. If you want a doorway into all of this that stays rational and low on mysticism, that's the one I'd point to: **train the camera, then trust the footage, then check it against the instruments.** The rest of this piece builds out each of these threads.

## Part 2 — The physics leg: memory, inertia, feedback

The "mud vs spring" idea, done properly, is a question about **memory kernels** — how a system's past enters its present through a weighted integral over its own history. Two intuitions compete for what such a memory *is*: control-theoretic **feedback** (the past sets a gain) or Newtonian **inertia** (the past gives you mass). They are usually treated as interchangeable. They aren't.

The result, in one paragraph: a purely dissipative, forgetting memory (a positive kernel that just decays) produces, at low frequency, an effective mass with the **wrong sign** — it cannot give you stable inertia. To get genuine inertia the memory has to have a **reactive, storing component** — formally, its velocity kernel must have a *negative lobe*, which is what a hidden mode that stores-and-returns looks like once you integrate it out. And the induced "mass" vanishes when that hidden mode becomes very fast, leaving only feedback. So *inertia and feedback are the reactive and the dissipative parts of one memory* — and modeling any history-dependence (a habit, an accumulated bias) as "inertia" quietly assumes a storage mechanism that plain forgetting does not have.

The full derivation — with the frequency-domain algebra, a reproducible `numpy` toy, and (importantly) a public list of the corrections a technical review sent back — is a separate piece:

[When Does Memory Become Mass? Inertia and Feedback in Systems with History]({{< relref "memory-inertia-feedback.md" >}})

I flag it here as a worked example of the whole stance: an intuition, made honest, turns out half-right, and you learn precisely which half.

## Part 3 — The consciousness question, with the rigor put back

The plain "movie vs screen" split is, more carefully, a split between two layers of mind:

- an **instrument** layer — structure, dynamics, memory, an update rule; the part that *processes and models*, including modeling itself; and
- a **witness** layer — the bare knowing in which the instrument's activity appears.

My working theory of the instrument layer is an ordinary dynamical one: mind as an embedded, memory-carrying control loop that builds a model of its world and of *itself as an agent in that world*, and updates that model to reduce surprise. In the current science this is essentially **Active Inference / the Free Energy Principle** — a self-evidencing agent. It is a theory of the instrument, and it is silent about the witness. That silence is honest, not a gap I'm hiding: it's the hard problem, conceded up front.

**The decomposed answer to "special or universal":**

- *Witness / phenomenality*: most contemplative traditions, and the more serious modern "consciousness is fundamental" positions (panpsychism, and process philosophy in the West), vote **universal and graded**. If phenomenality is fundamental, it is not what makes humans special. This is a metaphysical bet, and it is **not currently falsifiable** — I label it as such.
- *Instrument / structure*: here specialness is real but **graded**, and empirical. What is plausibly human-distinctive is a **depth of recursive self-modeling** (a loop that can represent and edit its own update rule) plus a **symbolic / cultural transmission channel** that carries the instrument's contents beyond a single body and a single life. Recursion and cumulative culture are the strongest human-distinctive candidates in the empirical literature; basic sentience and valence appear widely shared.

So: awareness plausibly universal; human specialness a structural achievement in the instrument, not a different witness.

### The traditions, used as a quarry (not as authorities)

I read the traditions as raw material for my own synthesis, not as scripture. Compactly, and honestly about where they map and where they don't:

| Tradition | What it puts in the *instrument* layer | What it points to as *witness* | Where its practice aims |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yogācāra Buddhism / Abhidharma | eight consciousnesses; "seeds" that ripen into experience and are re-perfumed by it; mind as a stream of discrete momentary events | bare knowing (rigpa) — **but with no owner** (*anatta*): knowing without a knower | see through it |
| Advaita Vedānta / Yoga | mind as a set of *modifications* leaving latent grooves that bias the next; layered sheaths | the changeless witness (*sākṣin* / *turīya*) in which the three states appear | dis-identify from it |
| Daoism | the constructed self; refinable vital structure | an empty mirror; the "pivot" where opposites stop binding | un-build it (*為道日損*) |
| Kashmir Śaivism | reality as consciousness progressively "contracting" into matter | awareness that is **full and self-aware**, not empty | recognize it |
| Neoplatonism / Kabbalah | soul/emanation as a source unrolling into a world (but *timelessly*, not as a process) | the One / the Infinite behind the emanations | return to it |
| Whitehead / panpsychism | reality as countless momentary "occasions," each with a felt inside | experience as the intrinsic nature of matter, everywhere | (argued, not practiced) |
| Modern science | Global Workspace, recurrent processing, Active Inference, attention-schema | **the hard problem — unexplained** | (measure it) |

Two families of genuine agreement are worth more than the rest:
- **The update loop.** The Yogācāra "seeds ripen into experience, experience re-perfumes the seeds," and the Yoga-sutra "each mental modification leaves a groove that biases the next," are close in *shape* to a history-carrying update operator. (Close in shape — not a precise mapping; the resemblance is at the level of "history biases the present," which is generic. Don't oversell it.)
- **The witness/instrument seam itself**, found independently again and again.

And the divergences I will *not* paper over — because forcing them to agree is exactly the trap of Part 1 §5:
- **Opposite goals.** My framework wants to *grow and refine* the instrument (a better model, a lighter agent). Buddhism and Daoism say the highest move is to *see through / un-build* it. Same machinery, opposite direction. I don't get to dissolve that with a clever reframing.
- **The witness's texture is disputed even among the traditions** — empty mirror vs full self-luminous presence. My framework has nothing to say, and shouldn't pretend to.
- **Cessation and timelessness** (a state with *no next moment*) have no analog in any dynamical theory at all.

## Part 4 — Where the map broke (and why I'm keeping it public)

I ran an adversarial review against the synthesis in Part 3. It landed several hits I've accepted:

1. **The instrument/witness split is partly my projection, not a universal discovery.** At least two traditions above actively refuse it (Madhyamaka: *both* layers are empty, so there's no witness-residue to file; Kashmir Śaivism: the "witness" is intrinsically active, which collapses the wall). I had bent them to fit my grid.
2. **"Independent convergence" was oversold.** Some of those traditions share direct historical lineage (Neoplatonism → Kabbalah), so they aren't independent votes; and an analyst who already holds the instrument/witness distinction will *find* it everywhere. Convergence is real but weaker than "everyone agrees."
3. **My own theory over-claims by breadth.** Saying it "cleanly owns the instrument layer" sounds strong, but a framework loose enough to rhyme with every tradition's language is loose *because* it's near-unfalsifiable — the breadth is a symptom, not a triumph. Active Inference has exactly this liability: it describes almost any adaptive system.
4. **Relabeling doesn't dissolve the hard sub-problem.** Even granting "the instrument is a generator, not the self," you still owe an account of what binds the instrument's many parts into *one* apparent point of view — and the traditions give opposite answers.

The deepest version of the critique: the whole activity of tabulating and unifying is itself a candidate for 所知障 — subtraction-work (see less, cling less) misfiled as addition-work (build a bigger, prettier model). The tell was subtle and worth stating: my *guard against over-unifying* had itself become a proud feature of the framework. The reflex was congratulating itself for resisting the reflex.

**What survives the razor:**
- the instrument-layer theory, held honestly as a near-unfalsifiable organizing frame whose real, defensible win is the *meta-problem* (explaining why a system would represent itself as having ineffable experience — not explaining the experience);
- the genuine, un-unifiable divergences (they're informative *because* they don't merge);
- and one actionable residue — the first-person practice, and its testable pairing with measurement.

Cut the map, keep the practice. The map was the scaffold that got me to that sentence; now it comes down.

## Part 5 — Building your own scientific framework: valid, or self-obstruction?

The natural worry, after Part 4: if map-building tips so easily into 所知障, is it a mistake to build a personal modern-scientific framework for this at all?

No — and the discriminator is a single variable: **which way authority flows between the map and the practice.**

- Framework **serves** practice (experience is the final court; when the map and the sitting disagree, the map yields; the theory is a raft) → valid.
- Practice **serves** framework (the theory becomes the goal, and is defended against experience) → 所知障.

Same activity; the direction decides whether it's medicine or disease. Three field-tests you can run on yourself in real time:

1. **The raft test.** Next time direct experience contradicts your framework, do you drop the framework or explain the experience away? Willing to drop it → medicine. Defending it → disease.
2. **The direction test.** Is refining the framework sending you *to* the practice, or *replacing* it? If model-building is *reducing* your actual practice time, it has flipped.
3. **The avoidance test** (the subtle one). "I understand it conceptually" is an easy hiding place from the discomfort of actually looking. Theory-building can be avoidance dressed as preparation.

And the language-specific caution, stated once more because it's the crux: the old maps were built *by practitioners, from first-person data*, and came with an explicit "drop this too." Modern-scientific language is third-person and objectifying, and has **no built-in drop-the-map move** — it tends to reify the model into the thing. So a scientific framework for practice is valid, but carries a *higher* reification risk, and you have to import the raft-discipline manually. (Part 4 is a live specimen of the risk: an anti-over-reach guard that mutated into a trophy.)

## Part 6 — The practice bridge: neurophenomenology

This is where the whole thing becomes something you can *do* and, in principle, *test*.

**The core idea** (Francisco Varela's, 1996): pair a rigorous *first-person* method with *third-person* brain measurement, and let each **constrain** the other. The direction of constraint is the point, and it's the same "authority flows practice→theory" from Part 5: first-person data disciplines the model; the model only points back at where to look.

**Why "trained" first-person reports.** Naïve introspection is unreliable — a well-known result in psychology is that people confidently report more than they can actually know about their own processing. The fix is not to abandon first-person data but to *train the instrument*: stabilize attention (the classic concentration practices) until reports become steady, fine-grained, and repeatable. This is exactly what the contemplative methods are *for*, read as instrument-calibration rather than doctrine.

**A method you can use solo** (micro-phenomenology, developed by Pierre Vermersch and Claire Petitmengin): after an experience, don't ask *what* (content), ask *how* — how did this arise, what immediately preceded it, what was its texture, how did it pass? You guide attention back to the pre-reflective *structure* of a single moment. It's a repeatable protocol, not a mood.

**What to attend to** — the vocabulary trained practitioners learn to report, i.e. the actual targets:
- the arising and passing of moments (their impermanence, watched directly, not believed);
- the *gap* between two thoughts;
- the difference between *being caught in* an experience and *witnessing* it;
- the *how / texture* of an experience rather than its content.

These are the instrument/witness seam from Parts 1 and 3, turned into concrete first-person targets you can check in your own experience — which is what makes this the *falsifiable-in-practice* end of the whole subject.

**That it's real science, not hand-waving** — three landmark results:
- first-person reports of a subject's own preparatory state were used to make otherwise-noisy EEG dynamics interpretable (Lutz, Lachaux, Martinerie & Varela, 2002);
- first-person descriptions of the aura preceding epileptic seizures were used to anticipate them (Le Van Quyen & Petitmengin, 2002);
- long-term meditators self-induce high-amplitude gamma synchrony during practice, giving a unique window on trained states (Lutz, Greischar, Rawlings, Ricard & Davidson, 2004).

If you want one rational, low-mysticism doorway into everything above: **train the camera, then trust the footage, then check it against the instruments.**

## Part 7 — Honest status and open problems

- The instrument-layer theory is a decent *organizing frame*, essentially today's best science (Active Inference), and by that same token **near-unfalsifiable as stated**. Its honest achievement is the meta-problem, not the hard problem.
- The **binding / combination problem** — what makes many parts into one point of view — is unsolved here, and shows up in the same shape in panpsychism and in my "nested self" picture. I don't have it.
- The **grow-vs-see-through** split (refine the instrument vs. dissolve it) is a real fork I haven't resolved and may not be resolvable by theory at all — it may be a choice, not a fact.
- The one part with a path to being **testable** is the neurophenomenology program. That's where I think the actual work is, and where a framework earns its keep instead of accreting.

If this mapped a small piece of the territory for you — especially the plain-language part — that's the point. It's a raft. Use it to get somewhere, then put it down.
